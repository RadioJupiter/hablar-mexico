How it works

This course teaches Spanish as it is spoken in Mexico, for use in conversation. You will hear complete, high-frequency phrases first, understand them, and then say them in the same kind of situation. Grammar is taught when it helps you build or choose a line, not as a separate subject.

What you do in each unit

Teaching comes before practice. A unit opens with an English explanation of the situation: who you are talking to, what is polite, and which forms matter. Then you hear a short model conversation, study the chunks it is built from, and only after that do you speak.

Comprehensible input. The model conversation is short enough to follow, with an English gloss under each line. Listen to the Spanish first, then read the gloss if you need it. You are not asked to invent Spanish you have not heard.

Chunks, not word-by-word translation. People do not build ¿Cómo estás? from a vocabulary list in real time. They reach for a ready piece. The Hear list is a set of those pieces. When a new pattern is needed (for example tú against usted, or how to say a price), it is explained on the spot.

Shadowing and role-play. Shadowing means playing the model again and speaking with it, matching the rhythm as closely as you can. The role-play then leaves your part blank, so you have to produce the line. That is the first time you are asked to speak without the words in front of you.

A check that mirrors the unit. The questions use the same greetings, orders, numbers, or directions you just studied. They are not a quiz on leftover grammar from somewhere else. The review sheet is one Letter-size page on the same material.

Mexican Spanish

Mexico uses ustedes for plural you. Vosotros is not used, and this course does not teach it. Tú and usted are both in play: usted is the safer first choice with people you do not know, with older people, and in more formal service; tú is usual with friends, many peers, and a good deal of casual food-stand talk. When you are unsure, start with usted. Informal expressions such as ¿qué onda? are taught because you will hear them constantly, and they are labeled informal so you do not use them with the wrong person.

The course prefers everyday Mexican words when they differ from other varieties: camión for a city bus, ¿mande? when you did not hear someone, ¿a cómo está? for a price at the market. Those choices are sourced in the dictionaries listed on the Sources page. Colorful slang that could not be verified there is left out.

What this is not

It is not a sentence-translation drill, a streak, or a tree of unlocked skills. It does not pretend that one English word has one Spanish word. It does not teach the Spanish of Spain as the default, and it does not invent Mexican slang. Browser voices are a stand-in for a person: useful for rhythm and a first model, not a substitute for listening to people in Mexico when you can.

Later work

Units 1–6 are the beginner start. Further beginner units, and a path toward more advanced conversation, are not in this course yet.
