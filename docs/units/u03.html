Unit 3 · The market and prices

Buying fruit, tortillas, or a small bag of chile is one of the most useful conversations you can have. This unit is numbers, pesos, and how to ask what something costs.

The market and prices

The market and prices

About 45 minutes · Situation: asking a price and buying by the kilo

Learn

At a market stall you greet the vendor, ask the price, say how much you want, and pay. The language is short. The numbers have to be solid, because a missed quince and cincuenta is an expensive mix-up.

Asking the price. ¿Cuánto cuesta? is “How much does it cost?” for one thing. ¿Cuánto cuestan? is for more than one. At a market stall in Mexico you will also hear ¿A cómo está? or ¿A cómo está el kilo? The Diccionario del español de México records ¿A cómo? as “at what price? how much does it cost?”, with the example ¿A cómo el kilo de jitomates? ¿A cómo son? is the same idea for things sold in a set or a pile.

The answer. Está a treinta pesos el kilo or A treinta el kilo. For a handful of items, Son veinte pesos. The pattern is está a + number + pesos for a unit price, and son + number + pesos for the total.

How much you want. ¿Me da un kilo de…? ¿Me da medio kilo de…? (half a kilo). ¿Me da dos kilos de…? Una bolsa de… is a bag. Unos plátanos is “some bananas” when you are not buying by weight.

Paying. Mexican money is the peso. You will hear amounts as veinte pesos, cincuenta, cien pesos, doscientos. If you only have a large bill, ¿Tiene cambio de quinientos? is “Do you have change for five hundred?” Aquí tiene is “here you are” when you hand money over.

Bargaining. Some market stalls will move a little on the price; shops and supermarkets generally will not. If you try, keep it light: ¿Me lo puede dejar en…? is “Could you let me have it for…?” Do not push if the answer is no. This unit practices asking and paying, not haggling.

Numbers you need. Learn 1–20 as a set, then the tens, then how to build the numbers in between: veintiuno, veintidós, treinta y uno, cuarenta y cinco. Watch the look-alikes: quince (15) and cincuenta (50); sesenta (60) and setenta (70); trece (13) and treinta (30). Cien is 100 before a noun (cien pesos); ciento appears in ciento uno and similar. Mil is 1,000. Medio is half.

All amounts in this unit are invented practice numbers, not current market prices.

Model conversation

A fruit stall in a market. The visitor greets the vendor, Don José. Prices are invented.

Visitor: Buenos días. ¿A cómo está el kilo de naranja? Good morning. How much is a kilo of oranges?

José: A veintidós pesos el kilo. Twenty-two pesos a kilo.

Visitor: ¿Me da dos kilos, por favor? Y medio kilo de limón. Could I have two kilos, please? And half a kilo of limes.

José: El limón está a treinta el kilo. ¿Algo más? The limes are thirty a kilo. Anything else?

Visitor: No, es todo. ¿Cuánto es? No, that's all. How much is it?

José: Son cincuenta y nueve pesos. That's fifty-nine pesos.

Visitor: ¿Tiene cambio de doscientos? Do you have change for two hundred?

José: Sí. Aquí tiene. Son ciento cuarenta y uno de cambio. Yes. Here you are. That's one hundred forty-one in change.

Visitor: Gracias, que le vaya bien. Thank you, all the best.

José: Igualmente, que le vaya bien. And to you.

Pronunciation

Count slowly out loud from one to twenty, then by tens to one hundred, and keep the vowels pure: uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte. The tens: treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa, cien.

The danger pairs differ by one syllable, not by a vague “ somehow.” Quince is KEEN-seh (two syllables). Cincuenta is cin-CUEN-tah (three). Trece is TREH-seh; treinta is TREN-tah. Sesenta has an s in the middle; setenta has a t.

Naranja is nah-RAHN-hah (the j is that back h sound). Limón is lee-MOHN. In much of Mexico limón is the everyday small lime.

The invented total in the model is honest arithmetic: two kilos of oranges at 22 pesos is 44, plus half a kilo of limes at 30 pesos a kilo is 15, which is 59. Change from 200 is 141.

Remember

Ask a stall price with ¿A cómo está el kilo? or ¿Cuánto cuesta? Totals use son + the number + pesos. Medio kilo is half a kilo. Do not mix up quince (15) and cincuenta (50), or sesenta (60) and setenta (70). Amounts in this unit are invented.

Watch me · shadowing

Play the model and listen for two different price shapes: a veintidós pesos el kilo (the unit price) and son cincuenta y nueve pesos (the total). Play it again and say the visitor's lines. When you come to the numbers, say them at full value, not as English numbers with a Spanish accent.

Check

These questions use only what this unit taught. Write the answers, then look at the key. All prices mentioned are invented practice numbers.

  1. Write two different ways to ask the price of a kilo of tomatoes (jitomate).
  2. How do you ask for half a kilo of limes?
  3. What is the difference between está a treinta pesos el kilo and son treinta pesos?
  4. Write the Spanish numbers for 15, 50, 60, and 70. What is easy to mix up?
  5. You need change for a 500-peso bill. What do you ask?
  6. In the model, the oranges are 22 pesos a kilo and you buy two kilos; the limes are 30 a kilo and you buy half a kilo. What is the total, in Spanish?

Practice

  1. Read aloud, slowly, 1–20 and the tens to 100, then these amounts: 22, 30, 59, 141, 200, 500.
  2. Write a four-line stall conversation of your own (greeting, price question, amount you want, total). Invent the prices and keep them consistent.
